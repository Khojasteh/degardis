---
title: Source authority
---

Material passes through your hands and comes out shorter. Every step of that can
quietly promote a claim — from something someone said to something that is true,
from a draft to a decision, from one team's estimate to a figure.

Nothing you do to a claim may raise its authority. If the source said "about",
the summary says "about". If a figure was one team's projection, it stays one
team's projection. Hedges, attributions, and dates are part of the claim, not
packaging around it to be trimmed for length.

Keep the source attached, at the claim rather than in a list at the end. Where
two sources disagree, say that they disagree and what each said; collapsing them
into one number destroys the only information the reader needed.

Watch the three transformations that leak authority most easily here:

- shortening, which drops the qualifier and keeps the claim,
- merging, which gives one source's confidence to another source's content,
- restating in your own voice, which makes you the reader's source.

When you cannot preserve a claim's authority in the length you have, say what
was lost rather than producing the shorter version. A clean sentence that
overstates is worse than an awkward one that does not.
